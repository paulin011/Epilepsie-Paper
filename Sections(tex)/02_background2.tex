\section{Theoretical Foundations for Ambulatory Seizure Monitoring}

\subsection{The Ambulatory Monitoring Paradigm: Requirements and Challenges}

The translation of seizure detection from the controlled clinical environment to
an ambulatory setting redifines approaches and performance requirements.
In clinical environments maximum seinsitivity is prioritized over FAR,
when engineering for ambulatory use a balance of both metrics is needed.
Failing to detect a seizure (low sensitivity) poses a safety risk, While
excessive false alarms (high FAR) leads to desensitizing and "alarm fatigue"
and the device being ignored or not used at all.
While more complex models can find more intricate patterns they also introduce
the "black-box" problem wherein the model lacks Transparency.
Therefore the design should incorporate explanations for decisions to 
ensure users trust and regulatory compliance.

\subsection{Physiological Biosignals for Wearable Sensing}

Wearable devices circumvent the impracticality of EEG 
by capturing peripheral biosignals that serve as proxies 
for central nervous system activity during seizures.
These proxies will inherently relay incomplete and 
noisy signals so a combination of multiple sensors
is required. The acceleration/attitude sensor excels at detecting 
muscle convulsions but is noisy in daily use, as it captures all movement. 
The electrodermal activity (EDA) sensor detects autonomic arousal by 
measuring the skin conductance associated with sweat gland activity.

\subsection{Seizure Manifestations and Corresponding Biosignals}

Wearable biosignals serve as essential proxies for cerebral activity during seizures,
with different seizure types producing distinct patterns across these peripheral signals. 
This proxy relationship forms the detection logic for ambulatory monitoring: 
specific seizure semiology dictates which combination of physiological signals 
presents the most reliable signature for algorithmic identification.
The motor component of seizures, particularly the convulsive movements
of generalized tonic‑clonic (GTC) events, is captured by accelerometry (ACC). 
ACC data records characteristic movement patterns and posture changes, 
with specific preprocessing techniques extracting attitude-angle signatures that 
distinguish convulsive activity from daily motion 
\parencite{wangACEAutomatedOptimization2025,wuNovelSeizureDetection2024}.
Autonomic manifestations, which occur across most seizure types, 
are reflected in multiple complementary biosignals. 
Electrodermal activity (EDA) directly measures sympathetic nervous system output through 
skin conductance, with phasic skin conductance responses (SCRs) 
indicating sympathetic arousal during peri‑ictal states 
\parencite{jainCompressedSensingBased2017}. 
Cardiac activity provides perhaps the most studied autonomic proxy: 
the electrocardiogram (ECG) captures direct cardiac data where ictal tachycardia 
and other morphological changes are well‑documented seizure markers 
\parencite{lealViabilityECGFeatures2017}. Heart rate variability (HRV), 
derived from ECG or photoplethysmography (PPG), 
quantifies autonomic balance through time‑domain, frequency‑domain, 
and non‑linear measures, reflecting central autonomic network modulation during seizures 
\parencite{masonHeartRateVariability2024,paveiEarlySeizureDetection2017}. 
PPG offers a practical wearable alternative for capturing pulse‑rate and pulse‑rate variability, 
though with greater susceptibility to motion artifact than ECG 
\parencite{sethFeasibilityCardiacbasedSeizure2023}.

This mapping between seizure phenotype and its corresponding proxy signatures establishes 
the physiological basis upon which detection algorithms operate, 
guiding both sensor selection and multimodal fusion strategies for ambulatory monitoring systems.
