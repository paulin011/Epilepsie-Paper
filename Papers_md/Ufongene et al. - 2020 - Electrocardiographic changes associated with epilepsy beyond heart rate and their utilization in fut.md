The ability to assess seizure risk may help provide timely warnings and more personalized treatment
plans for people with epilepsy (PWE). ECG changes are commonly observed in epilepsy which make
ECG a promising candidate to monitor seizure risk. Most ECG research in this domain has focused on
heart rate-related changes. However, several studies have identiﬁed a range of other peri-ictal ECG
parameter changes that may potentially prove useful for seizure detection and forecasting. Here, we offer
a systematic review of ECG changes in epilepsy outside of heart rate. We performed the systematic liter-
ature review according to PRISMA guidelines using key words related to ECG, SUDEP and epilepsy. We
identiﬁed and screened 502 abstracts, read 110 full papers, and included 24 papers in the ﬁnal review.
Our results suggest that PWE may be more prone to cardiac conduction abnormalities than healthy con-
trols. During interictal periods, PWE were more likely to have abnormal QTc intervals, ST segment abnor-
malities, elevated T Waves, early repolarization (ER), increased P Wave dispersion and PR intervals when
compared to controls. Apart from these baseline abnormalities, changes during the pre-ictal and ictal
states have been reported, with arrhythmias, QTc prolongation and ST segment changes being the most
common. A better understanding of these state-dependent changes may afford less-cumbersome and
less-stigmatizing epilepsy monitoring tools in the future.

1. Introduction

This review provides a survey on epilepsy associated electrocar-
diographic (ECG) changes outside of heart rate, some of which may
be useful in developing seizure detection and forecasting methods.
Reliable methods to detect and forecast epileptic seizures may help
alleviate a major burden for epilepsy patients, (Landover, 2016)
and support clinicians in delivering more objective, personalized
treatments (Meisel and Loddenkemper, 2019). Fueled by long-
term data and novel analysis approaches, recent years have seen
a surge of such increasingly accurate methods to monitor, detect,
and predict seizures in epilepsy (Mormann and Andrzejak, 2016;
Kuhlmann et al., 2018). However, in order to ensure that seizure
detection and forecasting are not just feasible, but implemented
into day to day epilepsy care, easy to apply and monitor modalities
amenable to everyday use are crucial (Dumanis et al., 2017).
Assessing seizure risk has therefore for several decades also con-
sidered electrocardiography (ECG). In contrast to EEG and ECoG,
ECG can be measured with minimal complexity, non-invasively
and with less stigma, which facilitates implementation in broad

ambulatory use. The overwhelming amount of research using
ECG for such a purpose has thereby focused on heart rate and heart
rate-related variables (Bruno et al., 2018). Motivated by research
showing that heart rate increases at seizure onset (Eggleston
et al., 2014), increases in heart rate are, for example, currently used
to trigger responsive Vagus nerve stimulation aimed at preventing
or shortening epileptic seizures (Boon et al., 2015; Fisher et al.,
2016). Apart from seizure detection, changes in heart rate and
heart rate variability have also been tested in seizure forecasting
research (Kerem and Geva, 2005; Valderrama et al., 2012;
Fujiwara et al., 2016; Pavei et al., 2017).

While heart rate and heart rate-related ECG variables have long
been the focus of epilepsy research, producing many excellent
reviews (Bruno et al., 2018), other elements of the ECG and their
potential role for seizure detection and forecasting have remained
less explored. In a recent study, it was found that single-channel
ECG contained a comparable amount of information about the
pre-ictal state to scalp EEG, and in this study whole frequency
spectrum ECG assessment contributed to the pre-ictal state evalu-

2. Methods

We conducted a systematic review according to Preferred
Reporting Items
for Systematic Review and Meta-Analyses
(PRISMA) guidelines (Moher et al., 2009) (Fig. 1). Inclusion criteria
consisted of studies (1) written in English, (2) reporting random-
ized controlled trials, cohort studies or cross-sectional studies, or,
more generally, reporting ﬁndings from original research studies
(thus excluding editorials, reviews, etc.) (Fig. 1), (3) including a
report of at least one person with epilepsy (PWE) who was alive
at the time of the study, (4) mentioning ECG, and (5) reporting
on peri-ictal ECG changes. Consequently, we excluded papers not
in English, reviews, letters, editorials, conference proceedings, case
reports, postmortem and animal studies, and unpublished work.
We performed the literature search in PubMed, Cochrane, Embase
and Cinahl, including all articles published from 1993 until January
2019. CU, RE and a librarian CR, developed the search strategy. We
used a combination of keywords: SUDEP, epilepsy, seizure, ECG,
and various ECG features such as P waves, QRS complex, and QTc
interval. Example MeSH term keywords included ‘‘sudden unex-
plained death in epilepsy,
‘‘sudden death in epilepsy,” and
‘‘SUDEP.” Similarly, we used ‘‘epilepsy,” ‘‘epilepsies,” and ‘‘epilep-
tic,” among other search terms. Papers included for potential
review were managed on an internal reference library and an elec-
tronic data entry tool. Our initial search strategy yielded 502
papers. After reviewing titles and abstracts, 110 papers remained.
Following a review of these full texts, 24 papers ﬁt the inclusion
criteria. To select papers, two independent reviewers (CU and RE)
had to agree on the inclusion of each paper. If there was disagree-
ment, a third reviewer (CM) decided on the inclusion or exclusion
of the paper. Data variables included age, gender, number of study
participants, ictal state analyzed use of anti-seizure or cardiac
medications, and seizure type. We classiﬁed data that related to
the ECG ﬁndings for the peri-ictal states.

3. Results

The main goal of our work was to provide a systematic review
of ECG changes in epilepsy beyond heart rate-related changes.
Our systematic review according to the Preferred Reporting Items
for Systematic Review and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA, Fig. 1) guideli-
nes yielded 24 articles to be included for ﬁnal review (Table 1)
(Moher et al., 2009). A particular focus of our work was on changes
occurring prior to or during seizures which could potentially be
used as warning or detection signs, respectively, but also on any
additional changes occurring peri-ictally. We thus report changes
in four categories, interictal, pre-ictal, ictal, and post-ictal. Our
interictal group included 19 studies, the pre-ictal group contained
1 study, the ictal group contained 9 studies, and post-ictal changes
were reported in 3 studies. Table 1 provides a summary of the main
ﬁndings reported in the articles. In this table, we list the time def-
initions for the respective states for better comparison across stud-
ies and to account for the temporal change of ECG alterations with
respect to seizure onset.

3.1. Pre-ictal ECG changes (one study)

3.1.1. ST segment changes

Pre-ictal ECG changes were overall only rarely the focus of the
studies identiﬁed by our search. One study reported pre-ictal,
deﬁned as 4 minutes prior to seizure, ST segment depression
(Tigaran et al., 2003). Speciﬁcally, Tigaran et al. compared 22
patients with drug-refractory epilepsy to healthy patients. The
authors observed that ST segment changes were not seen in the
healthy patients control group, even with maximal exertion
(Tigaran et al., 2003).

3.2. Ictal ECG changes (nine studies)

3.2.1. QTc interval changes

QT abnormalities were by far the most commonly reported ECG
feature. Corrected QT (QTc) interval changes, predominantly QTc
prolongations, were reported frequently during ictal ECG record-
ings. Notably, many studies determined individual cut-offs for
marked QTc prolongation and QTc prolongation severity. Moseley
et al. reported signiﬁcant ictal QTc lengthening in 5.2% of patients
and in 19% of seizures (n = 29) (Moseley and Britton, 2014).
Brotherstone et al. reported all of their 39 patients to exhibit signif-
icantly longer ictal QTc intervals, compared to the pre-ictal period
(5 minutes prior to seizure onset), even if the overall interval was
still within normal limits (Brotherstone et al., 2010). Twenty-three
percent of patients experienced QTc prolongation (>512 ms).
Surges et al. reported abnormal QTc prolongation during the ictal
and post-ictal phases in 12% of their subjects (Surges et al.,
2010b). They also found benign arrhythmias in 56% of patients,
independent of seizure type. In another study, Surges et al. looked
at ictal cardiac features in 91 seizures from 19 PWE and 19
deceased SUDEP patients (Surges et al., 2010a). They found
seizure-related QTc prolongation of more than 10 ms in 48.3% of
seizures and in 63.16% of patients. Twenty-ﬁve seizures and 24
patients with signiﬁcant QTc prolongation were from patients
who died of SUDEP. QTc prolongation occurred in upper limits in
10.1% of seizures and 13.2% of patients in this group. This study
also reported QTc values below normal limits in one PWE and
two SUDEP patients. Lateralization of seizure onset did not affect
QTc prolongation (Surges et al., 2010a). Similarly, Surges et al. also
found 9 out of their 38 patients had pathologic QTc dispersion
(>50 ms) of which 5 were SUDEP patients (Surges et al., 2010a).

3.2.2. ST segment changes/T wave inversion

ST segment abnormalities were less frequently reported but
appeared more severe when they did occur. Opherk et al. com-
pared interictal to ictal cardiac rhythm and conduction abnormal-
ities in 102 seizures from 41 patients (Opherk et al., 2002). Out of
21.5% of seizures that showed ECG abnormalities, 6% could poten-
tially be serious, or not benign, and were not observed pre-ictally.
Potentially serious abnormalities included ST depression occurring
in ﬁve seizures from one of the 41 patients, and T wave inversion
occurring in one seizure (Opherk et al., 2002).

Overall, ECG abnormalities most commonly occurred during
generalized seizures, where they were prevalent in 35% of general-
ized seizures. Tigaran et al. found that 40% of 22 patients with
drug-refractory epilepsy experienced ST segment depression at
the offset of their seizures (Tigaran et al., 2003). In this study,
77% of seizures associated with ST segment depression occurred
during sleep. ST segment changes were also more frequently asso-
ciated with generalized seizures. The authors reported that ST seg-
ment changes were not common in patients without epilepsy, even
with maximal exertion during a treadmill test. They also investi-
gated comorbidities and found that duration of the seizure, sex,

3.2.3. QRS axis

Two studies looked at QRS axes changes in the ictal state.
Brotherstone et al. measured autonomic instability in subclinical
seizures by comparing ictal to interictal periods from 33 seizures
in 11 PWE (Brotherstone and McLellan, 2012). They analyzed the
cardiac index of parasympathetic activity (CIPA), which assesses
the vagal tone by comparing the incoming QRS signal to a previ-
ously acquired QRS template (Brotherstone and McLellan, 2012).
Speciﬁcally, CIPA measures vagal tone by observing ‘‘the increase
in pulse interval per unit increase in systolic pressure.” A low CIPA
score (1–4) represents low vagal tone. The authors found that dur-
ing the ictal period, generalized onset subclinical seizures were
associated with increased CIPA and localized seizures were associ-
ated with decreased CIPA compared to the interictal period.
Decreased CIPA was taken as an indication of a decreased vagal
tone and cardiac parasympathetic activity (Brotherstone and
McLellan, 2012). Rugg-Gunn et al. measured the frequency of ictal
arrhythmias over 18 months in 20 patients with refractory focal
seizures. Twenty percent (four) of their patients had periods of
asystole that required permanent pacemaker insertion. Of those
four patients, three had potentially fatal asystole. Most asystole
occurred toward the end of the seizure. One patient had supraven-
tricular tachycardia. The authors reported that the side of seizure
lateralization did not affect these results (Rugg-Gunn et al., 2004).

3.3. Interictal (19 studies)

3.3.1. QTc Interval

As with the ictal phase, QTc abnormalities were reported as
very common interictally. Lamberts et al. reported that PWE had
signiﬁcantly longer QTc intervals (405 ms, n = 185) during
seizure-free periods than patients without epilepsy (294 ms,
n = 178) (Lamberts et al., 2015a). Severe QTc prolongation was
found in 5% of PWE versus 0% of patients without epilepsy. Delogu
et al. compared QTc intervals in 20 healthy controls, 20 patients
with Dravet Syndrome (DS), and 20 patients with other epileptic
syndromes (Delogu et al., 2011). They found that, during a baseline
24-hour interictal recording, QTc intervals were not different
between the three groups (Delogu et al., 2011). Similarly, Krishnan
et al. found that mean QTc intervals were not different between
their three groups of 116 PWE, 40 patients with non-epileptic sei-
zures (NESs), and 39 patients with probable epilepsy (Krishnan and
Krishnamurthy, 2013). They also reported that mean QTc intervals
had a positive correlation with age and being female (Krishnan and
Krishnamurthy, 2013).

Sadrnia et al. compared interictal QTc intervals of 254 PWE to
254 healthy controls (Sadrnia et al., 2013). The authors found
34.4% of patients with seizures had a QTc interval in the normal
range (440 ms or less) while 12.6% of cases were on the border
of being classiﬁed as long QT (between 440 ms and 460 ms), and
13% had QT intervals above the deﬁnition of long QT (>460 ms).
These ﬁndings were compared to controls, of which 35% had a
QTc interval between 440 ms and 460 ms, and only 9.4% had a
QT interval greater than 460 ms. Syncope and sudden death risks
were not different between the two groups (Sadrnia et al., 2013).
De Sousa et al. reported similar ﬁndings when looking at interictal

PWE had, on average, longer QTc intervals during seizure-free
periods. One of their 62 patients exhibited pathologic QTc prolon-
gation during the study (de Sousa et al., 2017). Ergul et al. reported
that QT and QTc values were similar between 15 patients with DS
and 20 healthy controls when analyzing 24-hour interictal ECG
recordings (Ergul et al., 2013).

Several studies investigated antiseizure medication (ASM)
action and diet on cardiac activity. Stefani et al. analyzed effects
on sodium channel blocking ASM withdrawal on interictal cardiac
function in 36 patients with medically refractory epilepsy using
ten-minute epochs of awake, REM and non-REM sleep (Stefani
et al., 2013). ASM were completely weaned in 44.4% of their
patients. The authors found none of their patients had pathologi-
cally long QT intervals. QT intervals shortened during withdrawal
of sodium channel blocking ASM during wake periods but not dur-
ing sleep. No other changes in ECG parameters were found in asso-
ciation with ASM withdrawal (Stefani et al., 2013). Schimpf et al.
analyzed the effect of ruﬁnamide treatment on cardiac features
in 19 patients with Lennox-Gastaut and other epilepsy syndromes.
They found that QT intervals shortened following ruﬁnamide
administration. Speciﬁcally, Tpeak-Tend intervals, deﬁned as the dif-
ference of the interval of the Q wave to the peak of the T wave and
the interval of the Q wave to the end of the T wave, were shorter.
(Schimpf et al., 2012).

Ozdemir et al. analyzed the effects of Keto Diet (KD) on seizure
suppression and cardiac function in 70 patients with epilepsy,
focusing at P wave duration and dispersion, QTc dispersion and
Tpeak-Tend intervals (Özdemir et al., 2016). The authors reported
that all parameters except for P wave dispersion increased after
12 months on the KD, although these increases were not statisti-
cally signiﬁcant. Overall, KD had no strong effect on cardiac func-
tion and repolarization (Özdemir et al., 2016). Akalin et al.
reported no difference between 28 patients with newly diagnosed
epilepsy, 34 patients under antiepileptic drug treatment, and 52
healthy controls in terms of QTc intervals (Akalin et al., 2003).

Indication for an association with QT changes and epilepsy has
also come from studies investigating cardiology patients primarily.
Garson et al. performed a community study looking at low and
high-risk patients with Long QT Syndrome (LQTS). Of 287 patients
investigated, 10% had seizures (Garson et al., 1993).

Interictal QT dispersion abnormalities appeared to be a com-
monality in PWE throughout many studies reviewed. Ergul et al.
reported that PWE (n = 15) had a higher QT or QTc dispersion than
controls (n = 20) (Ergul et al., 2013). Kolsal et al. found that at base-
line, 50% of patients in the drug refractory epilepsy group (n = 20)
and 25% of patients with drug-controlled epilepsy (n = 20) had
abnormal QTc dispersions, deﬁned as greater than 50 ms (Kolsal
et al., 2014). Statistically signiﬁcant QTc dispersion lengthening
was only observed in patients with drug-refractory epilepsy
(Kolsal et al., 2014). Surges et al. reported pathologic QTc disper-
sion in about 90% of PWE (n = 19). Conversely, they also found
QTc values below normal
limits in three out of 89 seizures
(Surges et al., 2010a). Akalin et al. also demonstrated that QTc dis-
persion values were signiﬁcantly increased in PWE compared to
healthy controls. ASM treatment did not seem to affect QT disper-
sion (Akalin et al., 2003).

found to be the only signiﬁcant predictor of these ST segment

PWE had signiﬁcantly higher rates of ER (34%, n = 185) compared

matched controls, although these results were still within normal
limits (de Sousa et al., 2017).

3.3.4. QRS axis/QRS interval

QRS abnormalities did not seem to be common in PWE interic-
tally according to our search. Krishnan et al. observed that PWE
had a more leftward leaning QRS axis but found no change in over-
all QRS interval duration (Krishnan and Krishnamurthy, 2013). The
authors also compared these features according to seizure types
and found no difference between generalized and focal seizures
for QRS and PT intervals as well as T waves (Krishnan and
Krishnamurthy, 2013). De Sousa et al. reported similar ﬁndings
with no differences in QRS interval when analyzing interictal ECG
of PWE (n = 62) and age- and sex-matched controls (n = 57) (de
Sousa et al., 2017). Ergul et al. investigated ECG abnormalities in
patients with DS (n = 15) and found no abnormalities in QRS axis
and QRS intervals (Ergul et al., 2013). Similarly, Akalin et al.
reported no differences in QRS duration or QRS axis when compar-
ing PWE (n = 62) to controls (n = 52) (Ergul et al., 2013).

3.3.5. Arrhythmias

Rugg-Gunn et al. measured the frequency of arrhythmias in 20
patients with refractory focal seizures (Rugg-Gunn et al., 2004).
Twenty-one percent of patients had periods of asystole and 75%
of these patients had potentially fatal asystole. Garson et al. ana-
lyzed risk factors for 287 patients with LQTS, a feature seen in
PWE. Beyond the ﬁnding that 10% of patients with LQTS had sei-
zures, ventricular arrhythmias were seen in 16% of initial routine
ECGs of patients with LQTS and atrioventricular blocks occurred
in 5% of overall patients (Rugg-Gunn et al., 2004). Lamberts et al.
investigated whether causes of SCA differed between 18 patients
who died of SUDEP and had ventricular tachycardia (VT) or ventric-
ular ﬁbrillation (VF), 54 PWE without VT/VF and 470 patients with
VT/VF without epilepsy (Lamberts et al., 2015b). They found that
myocardial infarction was the most common cause of VT/VF in
PWE.

For patients who died of SUDEP, VT/VF occurred at a younger
age (57 vs 64 years), occurred closer to home (89% vs 58%), and
was less likely to be wittnessed (72% vs 89%) compared to living
epilepsy controls. They also reported the prevalence of congenital
and inherited heart diseases to be higher in patients who died from
SUDEP than in living controls with epilepsy.

It should be noted that these two studies (Rugg-Gunn et al.,
2004; Lamberts et al., 2015b) collectively report on different at-
risk populations: patients with focal onset seizures with auto-
nomic linked asystole or bradycardia, patients with intrinsic heart
disease and patients where arrhythmias or asystole may have
resulted from respiratory-linked events. Thus, any evidence of T
wave variability or cardiac microﬁbrosis linked to seizures may
help to better disentangle the cause and attribute the risk for
arrhythmias.

3.4. Post-ictal (three studies)

3.4.1. QTc abnormalities

Post-ictal abnormalities as a whole were less often analyzed
than other states. However, in line with other studies, QTc abnor-
malities also seemed prevalent post-ictally. Surges et al. reported
abnormal QTc shortening to occur in 68% of PWE immediately
post-ictally (n = 25) (Surges et al., 2010b). The authors found this
change to occur signiﬁcantly more often in secondarily generalized
tonic-clonic seizures (Surges et al., 2010b). In another study, Surges
et al. found QTc shortening ﬁve minutes after seizure cessation in
two out of 19 SUDEP patients (Surges et al., 2010a). The authors
reported cardiac arrhythmias in nine out of their 38 patients which
included PWE and SUDEP patients. These arrhythmias were
considered benign and included premature atrial beats (Surges
et al., 2010a).

4. Discussion

We provide an overview of peri-ictal ECG changes beyond heart
rate. The effects of seizures on different subsystems of the auto-
nomic nervous system (ANS), including cardiac function, have been
shown for various seizure types (Mirsky and Van Buren, 1965;
O’Regan and Brown, 2005). Assessing these cardiac function
changes might provide insight into the pathophysiology of epilep-
tic seizures and could help identify times when seizure risk is high.
While heart rate and heart rate-related variables have long been
the focus of extensive epilepsy research for these reasons (Bruno
et al., 2018), other features in ECG might be equally or even more
indicative of an impending or occurring seizure and other seizure
complications, most important being SUDEP (Meisel and Bailey,
2019). More comprehensive assessments of ECG might help to
develop better methods for automatic seizure detection or seizure
forecasting. ECG based methods might help, to a certain extent,
overcome some limitations of EEG or ECoG and be more applicable
for everyday use in the ambulatory setting (Dumanis et al., 2017).
Furthermore, a better understanding of the cardiac effects associ-
ated with seizures might help to gain important insights into the
pathophysiology of seizures, insights that could potentially also
help to better characterize patients at risk for SUDEP.

Following the PRISMA guidelines for systematic reviews (Moher
et al., 2009) (Fig. 1) lead to the identiﬁcation of 24 papers (Table 1)
and demonstrated that the literature focused on these alternative
ECG alterations is comparably limited. In particular the number
of studies focusing on pre-ictal ECG changes was very low, despite
the relevance that such changes might have in informing potential
seizure forecasting methods. Often pre-ictal intervals were consid-
ered only for comparison to ictal states. A distinct comparison to
how ECG conduction properties might already change during the
pre-ictal state, however, was often not performed. Overall, ECG
changes broadly related to cardiac repolarization (e.g. QT and ST
intervals) appear to be the most reported or studied and were
essentially reported for all states — pre-ictal, interictal, post-ictal
and ictal — in the literature. In comparison to control groups, these
changes also appear to be more prevalent in PWE.

The most common cardiac conduction abnormalities involved
QTc prolongation and QTc dispersion. Most studies had different
deﬁnitions for what was considered lengthening beyond normal
limits, but studies concluded QTc interval prolongation to be a
common abnormality in PWE. Several studies also reported the
effects of antiseizure medications on QTc interval and found that
they can also contribute to either lengthening or shortening. For
instance, ruﬁnamide and weaning off sodium channel blockers
administration shortened QT intervals (Stefani et al., 2013;
Schimpf et al., 2012) indicating that uncontrolled epileptogenicity,
in the presence or absences of seizures, may independently con-
tribute to autonomic changes. One study reported QTc shortening
immediately after seizure offset; it is thus possible that seizures
and the pre-ictal state might cause QTc prolongation and upon
recovery, QTc shortening towards baseline. Similarly, several stud-
ies reported ST segment and T wave abnormalities. PWE were
found to have a higher prevalence of these abnormalities and a
higher risk of SCA as a result.

Similarly, other conditions besides seizures are known to cause
QTc prolongation. For example, exercise (Nie et al., 2019) and
migraine (May et al., 2015) have been shown to be associated with

(for example in EEG) has long been emphasized in epilepsy
(Mormann et al., 2007). Thus, when considering any of the
reported ECG changes, for example for seizure detection or fore-
casting methods, it will also be important to consider their speci-
ﬁcity and sensitivity, similarly to approaches based on EEG or
ECoG.

Seizures may arise or spread to areas of the brain that control
autonomic function and hence cause autonomic alteration, such
sympathetic or parasympathetic activation. These autonomic
changes, which also play a role in SUDEP, may consequently man-
ifest as changes in cardiac conduction (de Sousa et al., 2017; Ergul
et al., 2013). Autonomic dysfunction furthermore contributes to
other heart rate independent ECG changes including changes in
QT intervals and ST segments (Stefani et al., 2013; Schimpf et al.,
2012). Also, ketogenic diet might affect repolarization properties
(Özdemir et al., 2016). While changes in heart rate, especially sinus
tachycardia, seem to be the most common manifestation, other
abnormalities do occur (Eggleston et al., 2014) and could ideally
be picked up by future diagnostics based on ECG. Overall, while
the causes underlying ECG changes may be multifaceted and
should be carefully considered, the association of such ECG
changes with epileptic seizures, in particular changes related to
cardiac repolarization, suggests that ECG features beyond heart
rate might be useful to consider for future seizure detection and
forecasting methods.

Likewise, it is also important to note the different interactions
that seizure type and etiology may have with the ECG. While Krish-

abnormalities, other studies suggest generalized seizures are asso-
ciated with greater ECG abnormalities than focal seizures. In par-

abnormalities in their generalized onset group (Tigaran et al.,
2003). Similarly, studies by Surges and Fialho report evidence to
suggest those with secondarily generalized seizures have lower
overall cardiovascular ﬁtness (Fialho et al., 2017; Surges et al.,

Our results need to be interpreted in the context of method-
ological considerations. First, there is a possibility that some stud-
ies were missed because they did not ﬁt with our inclusion and
exclusion criteria. We utilized a variety of terms in our search
strategy to help screen for related manuscripts, however, it is pos-

our search even further. Second, our initial title and abstract
screening process may have excluded papers that discussed infor-
mation we were interested in the full text but not in the abstract.

changes around the time of a seizure, some studies showed con-
ﬂicting changes. Some studies looked at speciﬁc epilepsy syndrome
or seizure type and their results may not be generalized to all
patients.

In summary, our literature search indicates that there are pro-
nounced changes in the ECG in the peri-ictal state and between
PWE and control groups that go beyond heart rate changes.
Changes related to cardiac repolarization appear to be most promi-
nently reported and are seen at baseline, before, during and imme-
diately after seizures. Given that seizures are known to induce ECG
changes, it is possible that such ECG alterations can be used to
determine autonomic changes occurring prior to seizure onset or
during seizures. The pathophysiology underlying these effects
appears to be still incompletely understood. As discussed, it is
important to consider variables such as differences between gener-
alized and focal seizures (Table 1) and effects of ASM. Still, with
more research, the use of ECG to detect and potentially forecast
seizure onset
in selected patients might help to add less-
cumbersome and less-stigmatizing diagnostic tools for epilepsy
management, and ECG might serve as an additional sensing modal-
ity in seizure patients, adding independent information on seizure
risk and risk from seizures.
